== v0.1.0 — 2026-09-26

=== Added

* `PasswordPolicy::UseCase` — the three CNIL tiers and their minimum entropies
  (80, 50 and 13 bits), each naming the complementary measures it assumes.
* `PasswordPolicy::Policy` — composition rules, minimum and maximum length,
  passphrase word count, a denylist, and `#entropy` / `#satisfies_use_case?`.
  Three presets matching the CNIL's own worked examples: `.composed`, `.long`
  and `.passphrase`.
* `PasswordPolicy::Violation` — reasons as enum values, with `#i18n_key`.
* `PasswordPolicy::Entropy` — entropy of an alphabet and of a passphrase.

=== Extracted from kemal-auth, not copied

The rules came from `aloli-crystal/kemal-auth`, where they were bound to one
framework's auth stack. Four things changed on the way out:

* *The CNIL model.* Those rules implemented the 2017 recommendation — a minimum
  length plus four mandatory character classes. The 2022 revision replaced that
  with entropy tiers on purpose, to allow policies suited to their use case.
  A 14-character policy with no special character is now equally acceptable,
  and easier on users.
* *Messages are no longer sentences.* `validate` returned hardcoded French
  strings, which is what stopped the rules being reusable. It now returns
  `Violation` values and an `i18n_key`.
* *No hashing.* BCrypt hashing stayed behind, where it belongs: mixing policy
  with storage is what tied the original to Kemal.
* *The length limit is in bytes.* The 72-byte ceiling exists because BCrypt
  truncates there — and it truncates bytes, not characters. A 40-character
  accented passphrase is 80 bytes and already past it. The original checked
  characters.

=== Notes

* `Policy.composed` computes to 79.6 bits, a rounding short of the 80 the CNIL
  states for that example: 26 + 26 + 10 + 37 = 99, and 12 × log2(99) = 79.6.
  The figure is left as the arithmetic gives it; `.long` or `.passphrase` clear
  the 80-bit tier.
* `#entropy` is the entropy of the policy, not of a chosen password. Scoring an
  individual password requires guessability estimation, which the CNIL names as
  the better approach while declining to set a threshold for it.
